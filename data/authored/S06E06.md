---
topic: Greedy
subtopic: Raise the smallest digit first
time: $O(d \log d)$ per test case with $d \leq 7$ digits
space: $O(d)$
---
Raising a digit from $x$ to $x+1$ multiplies the product by $\frac{x+1}{x}$ — a ratio that **shrinks as $x$ grows**: $1 \to 2$ doubles, $2 \to 3$ gives $1.5$, $8 \to 9$ gives only $1.125$. So each increment is best spent on the **smallest** digit available.

A digit of $0$ is the extreme case: the product is zero until every $0$ is lifted, and since $0$ is also the smallest digit, the greedy handles it without a special rule.

The bound that makes this trivial to execute: $N \leq 10^6$ has at most $7$ digits, so the total number of useful increments is at most $7 \times 9 = 63$. Any $K$ beyond that leaves every digit at $9$. So the loop runs $\min(K, 63)$ times regardless of $K$ reaching $10^6$ — no need to reason about huge budgets.

A min-heap keeps "smallest non-nine digit" cheap, though with seven digits a plain scan would do.

Sample case 2, $N = 2221$ with $K = 3$: the $1$ is raised to $2$, then two of the $2$s become $3$s, giving digits $\{2,2,3,3\}$ and a product of $36$. Spending all three on one digit instead would give $\{2,2,2,4\}$ and only $32$ — spreading across the smallest digits wins.

Sample case 1 raises a lone $1$ five times to $6$. Sample case 3 has $K = 0$ and simply multiplies $1 \times 2 \times \cdots \times 6 = 720$.

## Edge cases

- **Raising the smallest digit first is optimal**, because incrementing $d$ scales the product by $(d+1)/d$, which is largest when $d$ is smallest — so a min-heap drives the greedy with no lookahead.
- **A zero digit is the extreme case of that rule.** The product is $0$ while any digit is $0$, and lifting it to $1$ is worth more than any other increment, which the heap does automatically by putting $0$ first.
- **Digits cap at $9$**, so the loop must stop when the smallest digit is already $9$ — otherwise a large $K$ would spin forever. With at most seven digits, $63$ increments saturate everything.
- **$K$ may be enormous** relative to the digits ($K \leq 10^6$ against $N \leq 10^6$), so the saturation guard is doing real work, not just tidying up.
- **$K = 0$** leaves the number alone and the answer is the plain digit product (the third row, $1 \cdot 2 \cdots 6 = 720$).
- **Leading zeros are absent from the input**, and the greedy never creates one since it only increases digits.
- **The digits come straight from the byte token** with `c - 48`, avoiding a decode per case.

## Dry run

Second sample: $N = 2221$, $K = 3$.

| step | digits (as a multiset) | product |
|---:|---|---:|
| start | $1, 2, 2, 2$ | 8 |
| raise the $1$ | $2, 2, 2, 2$ | 16 |
| raise a $2$ | $2, 2, 2, 3$ | 24 |
| raise a $2$ | $2, 2, 3, 3$ | **36** |

Output `36`. Spending all three on one digit instead ($1 \to 4$) would give $4 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 32$.
